This project explores how artificial intelligence can be used to recognize and interpret human hand gestures in real time. I trained a model to identify four different gestures: Thumbs Up, Peace Sign, Wave Hello, and Aloha. When a user performs one of these gestures in front of their camera, the model analyzes the input and predicts which gesture it recognizes. By showing the percentage of gesture probability. The inspiration behind this project comes from the growing use of gesture based interactions in most of the social media updates over the past year. As AI continues to advance, we are seeing more products and applications that allow people to interact with technology through natural movements and commands rather than relying solely on traditional keyboards, touchscreens, or voice commands. People with arthritis, tremors, limb differences, or limited mobility might not be recognized. So this project is a small demonstration of how AI could contribute to more intuitive and interactive experiences, and also a reminder of what it can miss. -Wilfred

When I began this assignment, I approached machine learning primarily as a tool that could be trained to recognize patterns in images. For this project, I created an image classification algorithm using Google's Teachable Machine to distinguish between Apple earphones and Beats headphones. I collected photographs of both objects from different angles and perspectives, sometimes holding them in front of the camera and other times including myself in the image. After training the model, I found that it could successfully distinguish between the two types of headphones fairly well. However, I became more interested in what happened when I removed both objects from the camera's view. Even though neither Apple earphones nor Beats headphones were present, the model continued to classify the image as one of the two. It classified my person as beats headphones. This unexpected result made me question what it actually means for an algorithm to recognize something. In connection with Joy Buolamwini's Unmasking AI, the experience encouraged me to think more critically about the relationship between training data, human decisions, and the limitations of artificial intelligence. It is basing its predictions on the data it is trained on, and that limited perspective is hard to replace nuanced lived experiences. - Eunice

Throughout the course, we have studied how past standards for the behavior of computers and machines we program has become ingrained into our modern technological landscape. While reading through Buolamwini's Unmasking AI, I saw resonances in the unnatural and incomplete defaults mentioned in chapter 5 as my camera adjusted its settings to match myself and the background I inhabit. Each change is a trained setting to match the preexisting appearance data imprinted into the software. When I fed images of the objects I was training the model on, a set of keys and a bottle of hand sanitizer, I included myself in several pictures. I knew that other students may test the data sets while they were in frame, so I wanted to mirror this potential behavior. Simultaneously, however, I was aware that by introducing these pictures, I was pushing for a image set that only I could replicate, while also ingraining new biases into the system. When I leaned backwards, the model spiked higher for the hand sanitizer category, while leaning closer consistently made the model detect a set of keys. These form two sides of the bias, hidden but encoded within the training data. It made me consider my training data, as I was only able to recognize this difference through testing the model in separate contexts. Throughout this project I have found that as social structures narrow in on a view of normality, we have to rigorously examine the patterns that coalesce into a coded gaze, and find the biases where we aren't looking. -Shengdong
