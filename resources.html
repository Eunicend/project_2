Key ideas

Implicit bias and intersectionality help us examine how different people are perceived and how lived experiences are shaped.

Intersectionality
Patricia Hill Collins and Sirma Bilge (2016) describe intersectionality as a way to understand the complexity of people and human experiences. They explain that social inequality is shaped not by just one division, such as race, gender, or class, but by many that work together. I agree with this idea because intersectionality helps me see how a person can fit into more than one category or social group. All the intricate parts of someone can coexist and work together to represent one whole.
Implicit bias
The American Psychological Association describes implicit bias, also called implicit prejudice or implicit attitude, as an attitude toward a social group that a person may not consciously recognize. My understanding is that implicit bias is a subconscious belief we hold about a person or group. We may not even be aware of it, but it can affect our actions, decisions, and treatment of others.

A personal reflection

Viewing people through this lens helps me understand myself, the people around me, and people I may not know. We are all complex, even if our experiences are different. That is why I think it is important not to assume one part of someone's identity tells their whole story.

The Harvard Implicit Association Test (IAT) can help people notice associations they may not have realized they had. When I took the gender test, I was asked to sort STEM careers and subjects as well as humanities. It made me think about how people can be more likely to associate men with STEM careers and women with the humanities. I think this is partly because, for a long time, women were not welcomed or encouraged in those spaces. As a black woman in STEM myself, I have experienced imposter syndrome partially due to not seeing as many faces like mine in STEM. It wasn't until I immersed myself into spaces that encouraged diversity in STEM that I felt belonging, and a renewed passion for my field.

I also thought about the Navajo women who made microchips at the Shiprock plant. Fairchild compared their work to weaving, a common traditional craft performed by many Navajo women. I can see how that comparison might connect their work to knowledge they already had, but I also wonder whether it diminished the technical skill of what they were doing. They deserved recognition and compensation for their work. This example makes me think about who gets seen as an expert, and how implicit bias can affect what we notice, value, and reward.

Questions to consider

  • What assumptions did you notice while taking the test or reading these definitions?
  • How might someone's overlapping identities change the way they experience a situation?
  • When does comparing someone's work to a familiar craft recognize their skill, and when might it minimize it?
  • What evidence, perspectives, or voices would help you examine your first reaction?
